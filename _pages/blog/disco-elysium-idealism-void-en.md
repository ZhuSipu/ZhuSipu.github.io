## I. Worldview

The epigraph above is an old letter. Its writer is Dora Ingerlund; its recipient is Harry Du Bois. It was written before their relationship broke apart, in an intimate voice that still believed shared life could continue. Later, poverty, alcoholism, self-destruction, emotional collapse, and the traumatic memory of a terminated pregnancy gradually corroded that relationship. Dora left Harry, and she also left Revachol, the place where the story unfolds.[^1]

{% include figure.liquid path="/assets/img/user/blog/disco/02.jpg" alt="Harry pleading with Dora in a dream before she leaves" caption="Harry pleading with Dora in a dream before she leaves" loading="lazy" zoomable=true %}

The fictional world of <em>Disco Elysium</em> is called Elysium. It is not a continuous planet but a world made up of several landmasses, or isolas, and the seas around them. Between the isolas lies the Pale. The Pale is an anomalous space that erodes matter, memory, and cognition, so the geography, transportation, knowledge, and history of this world all feel unstable.[^2] Revachol is located on the isola of Insulinde, one of Elysium's major regions, and Revachol itself is among its most important cities. It was once a center of colonial expansion and imperial wealth. The old order collapsed under royal corruption, social inequality, and political crisis. The communards, the game's term for communists, launched the Antecentennial Revolution and established the Revachol Commune, which was then crushed by a coalition of foreign powers. Since then, Revachol has lost its sovereignty and has remained under Coalition administration and financial control. By the time of the game, Elysium is broadly governed by a liberal capitalist order maintained by the Coalition and the Moralintern, an order that emphasizes markets, procedure, stability, and the suppression of radical politics.[^3]

Harry is a middle-aged detective. At the beginning of the game, he wakes up in a hostel room in the Martinaise district of Revachol, severely hungover and amnesiac. He cannot remember his name, his occupation, his badge, or his gun. The player later confirms that Harry belongs to Precinct 41 of the Revachol Citizens Militia and has been sent to investigate a hanged body in the hostel courtyard. The case unfolds during a harbor strike and involves the dockworkers' union, the Wild Pines company, mercenaries, striking workers, fishing village residents, the police system, and the Coalition order of occupation. The process of identifying the victim, determining the cause of death, and finding the killer also reveals the class structure of Martinaise, its colonial legacy, corporate power, and the political remnants left behind by a failed revolution.[^4]

{% include figure.liquid path="/assets/img/user/blog/disco/03.jpg" alt="Harry waking up in his room at the Whirling-in-Rags" caption="Harry waking up in his room at the Whirling-in-Rags" loading="lazy" zoomable=true %}

The game is filled with people who embody different ideological positions. Evrart Claire, the dockworkers' union boss, advances his own power in the name of the working class and collective interest. Joyce Messier, the representative of Wild Pines, acts as an agent of capital and transnational commercial order, habitually explaining everything through markets, transactions, and rational negotiation. René Arnoux, an old veteran, clings to the glory of the former monarchy and embodies the remnants of nationalism, patriarchal order, and conservative authority. Measurehead, the racist gatekeeper, radicalizes ideas of body, blood, and hierarchy, forming a comic yet dangerous variant of fascist discourse. Martinaise, then, is not a political vacuum. It is better understood as a site where political ideals, material interests, and historical residues are tangled together: communards still speak of revolution among ruins; ultraliberals understand the world as price and exchange; fascists project personal failure onto the collapse of national and gender order; moralists delay real change in the name of neutrality and reason.[^5]

Harry's ideological orientation is also a game mechanic. If the player repeatedly chooses communist, fascist, moralist, or ultraliberal positions in dialogue, the system records these tendencies through skill prompts and the Thought Cabinet. The Thought Cabinet is a system for recording, internalizing, and altering Harry's ideas; it turns political positions, psychological trauma, and absurd thoughts alike into "thoughts" that can be researched and that affect the character's stats.[^6]

On the night of the third day, Harry can enter one of the political vision quests; in a single playthrough, only one such political branch can be fully pursued. In the communist branch, Harry can accept the task "Get yourself organised" and search Martinaise for communards who are still alive. He meets the graffiti artist Cindy and the union member Mañana, then enters a secret meeting in an apartment at night. Inside are only two student communards, Steban and Ulixes. They claim to be running the most advanced materialist reading group in Martinaise, though in practice there are only two members. When Harry enters the room, they are trying to build a tower out of matchboxes and repeatedly failing. Harry joins them in rebuilding the matchbox tower. The three of them seem to be trying to prove, through this fragile little structure, that as long as people still believe in communism together, some material miracle remains possible. The tower does briefly stand, but soon collapses again.[^7]

The matchbox tower is modeled on Tatlin's unbuilt Monument to the Third International. Tatlin's Tower emerged from the atmosphere of revolutionary avant-garde art in Lenin's time. Designed by Vladimir Tatlin around 1919-1920 for the Communist International, it imagined a vast spiral structure of iron and glass that would symbolize revolution, industrial technology, and the future of world communism.[^8] It was never built, for reasons including the devastation of the Russian Civil War, scarcity of materials, and limited engineering capacity. After Stalin consolidated power, socialist realism gradually became the official artistic line, while experimental, abstract, and internationalist avant-garde tendencies such as constructivism were criticized as "formalism" and pushed to the margins.[^9] In this sense, the game's matchbox tower is not merely a joke about belief. It is also a miniature image of revolutionary idealism damaged, distorted, and made weightless by history.

## II. Ideology

After the matchbox tower collapses, disco keeps playing. This juxtaposition almost summarizes the historical feeling of <em>Disco Elysium</em>: revolutionary ideals have already failed in reality, but people do not therefore stop living, arguing, trading, remembering, and justifying themselves. The game's writing of ideology begins precisely from this world that continues to run after failure.

In the introduction to the <em>Disco Elysium</em> artbook, art director Aleksander Rostov wrote that he "grew up in the post-apocalypse. In the ruins of the former Soviet Union, it was a newborn land filled with arms deals, rapid financialization, and cowboy capitalists killing one another in public squares."[^10] The post-Soviet experience named here is not merely a geographic reference to the former Soviet region. It refers more broadly to a historical rupture in which old political ideals lost their force, marketization arrived as a shock, and both the state and individual identity had to be reorganized. It is within this break that the game transforms post-Soviet experience into an ideological field. Old slogans remain on walls, in conversations, and in veterans' memories; Wild Pines, mercenaries, the Coalition, financial receivership, and moralist order form the practical framework of corporate interest, technocratic governance, and the maintenance of stability.

By "idealism" in this essay, I do not mean a vague benevolence or moral enthusiasm. I mean a grand promise about the order of the future. During the Cold War, socialism and capitalism both offered such images, and both tried to prove that they represented a universal future. Socialism promised that through public ownership and planned coordination of production and distribution, a world without exploitation or scarcity could eventually be built: resources would flow according to need, labor would become free creation, equality and mutual aid would become ordinary social relations, and human beings would achieve all-around emancipation and shared flourishing within community. Capitalism promised that through markets and property rights, a world of continuous innovation and general prosperity would emerge: opportunities would be open to all, resources would be allocated more efficiently through competition, individuals could constantly break through limits and realize themselves, and richer lives would be possible through free choice and infinite possibility. Their directions differed, and in practice both contained oppression and contradiction, but both once organized imagination in the name of "the future."[^11]

{% include figure.liquid path="/assets/img/user/blog/disco/05.jpg" alt="Something beautiful is going to happen." caption="Something beautiful is going to happen." loading="lazy" zoomable=true %}

These idealist images have all encountered severe crises in historical practice. Compared with the Cold War period, the more obvious change today is that many societies no longer believe they possess a historical road toward a universal future. The grand historical narrative of socialism has weakened, shattered, or bureaucratized in many places; capitalism, too, less and less inspires the public through words like freedom, democracy, and the public good, and more often appears as technocratic governance, market logic, consumerism, performance pressure, and risk management. In other words, during the Cold War both sides at least retained some capacity to believe in themselves. In the post-Cold War world, many societies seem to have entered an age of realism, cynicism, and managerialism after the ebbing of ideals. This does not mean ideals have disappeared. It means they can no longer organize the direction of common life as stably as they once did.[^12]

Because its creators, worldbuilding, and emotional structure are all deeply marked by post-Soviet experience, some critics have called the game "the last letter sent by the Soviet Union" or "a love letter from the former Soviet Union."[^13] At The Game Awards in 2019, members of ZA/UM also thanked Repin, Makovsky, Viktor Tsoi, Marx, and Engels for their influence and political inspiration.[^14] These facts show that the left tradition is indeed an important background for <em>Disco Elysium</em>.

But that does not mean the game directly propagates a political answer. Communists see communism in the game, capitalists see capitalism in it, but that is only one part of what the game does. What makes <em>Disco Elysium</em> distinctive is that it consistently refuses to establish a stable value center. Communism, liberalism, fascism, and moralism conflict with one another in the game, but none of them is clearly installed as the "correct answer." The game romanticizes revolution and mocks revolution; it criticizes capitalism and shows the historical failure of idealism.

This complexity appears not only in the matchbox tower but also in the union, the communist dialogue options, and the ending. The game preserves an emotional attachment to failed revolution, yet refuses to romanticize left nostalgia. Evrart and the union are corrupt and manipulative. In the name of building a youth center, the union harms the interests of the fishing village and becomes entangled in drug trafficking and power deals. Many communist dialogue options are absurd, paranoid, and exaggerated, sometimes seeming to allude to historical violence: "the revolution was not thorough enough; they should have killed four million more people." These are not merely jokes. They remind the player that revolution is not naturally pure, and that left politics can become entangled with violence, dogma, power, and control. In this sense, <em>Disco Elysium</em> both preserves communism's emotional appeal as a failed ideal and presents the problems of left politics in organization, language, and power. This attitude is sharpened by the ending, where the final culprit is a communist veteran shaped by failed revolution, long solitude, and personal desire. He connects the game's complex attitude toward communism to its unconventional narrative structure.

> "In most games, failure means 'try again.' But in <em>Disco Elysium</em>, failure is the most beautiful part. The clumsiness of Harry messing things up, the shattering of his self-esteem, often brings visual expression and narrative depth more captivating than success. We spent a great deal of effort depicting this 'beautiful failure.'"
>
> "In most games, failure means 'try again.' But in Disco Elysium, failure is the most beautiful part. The clumsiness of Harry messing things up, the shattering of his self-esteem—it often brings a more captivating visual expression and narrative depth than success ever could. ## III. Narrative Philosophy

Iosef Dros, a man left on the margins by the failure of revolution, ultimately occupies the position of the "true culprit" in the detective story. It is here that the game's double dismantling of detective narrative and political narrative becomes especially clear. The ending of <em>Disco Elysium</em> rejects revelation in the traditional sense. Over the course of the week, the player investigates, moving among the lies of the union boss, the pressure of Wild Pines capital, and the anger of the striking workers. Many clues suggest that behind the case there may be a class struggle, an international conspiracy, or a political storm capable of changing the course of history. It is easy for the player to expect a rigorous, large-scale explanation.

Yet the final truth does not unfold according to that expectation. After layers of fog are peeled away, the player meets not the core figure in a network of power, but someone left behind at the edge of history. The killer hiding in the island fortress is not a strategist or mastermind, but a deserter from the failed Revachol Commune. He has hidden in a moldy bunker, endured more than forty years of loneliness and physical pain, watched the people of Martinaise through a sniper scope, and twisted that surveillance into a form of moral judgment that authorizes itself. When he sees the victim and Klaasje having sex in the room across the water, jealousy, loneliness, resentment at being forgotten by history, and residual political language become tangled together and finally produce the shot. It is not a revolutionary action and not part of any grand plan. It is violence produced by personal desire and failed history together. Thus revolutionary residue no longer appears here as organized force, but as the pathological fixation of an isolated individual.[^15]

{% include figure.liquid path="/assets/img/user/blog/disco/07.jpg" alt="Harry confronting the communist deserter on the island" caption="Harry confronting the communist deserter on the island" loading="lazy" zoomable=true %}

This forms the game's anti-climax. Anti-climax does not mean narrative failure. It means the work deliberately breaks the detective genre's expectation that the revelation of the mystery restores order. Traditional detective fiction usually follows a structure in which strict logic produces determinate truth. The murderer is identified, the chain of causality closes, and social order is symbolically repaired.[^16] <em>Disco Elysium</em> offers a different kind of ending: the killer is at the edge of the map, almost absent from the first several days of the game. He is not the center of the power network, but a remainder of historical failure. This design breaks the player's genre expectation. Truth does not restore order. It only shows that order itself was never stable. The case is produced by historical residue, class violence, accidental desire, and personal pathology.

Most mainstream narrative games still retain strong authorial control. Although the player appears to have choices, those choices often occur within branches already prepared by the author. Plot, reward systems, characterization, and endings frequently guide the player toward a "correct position." The player is not infinitely free to produce meaning; rather, the player understands and completes paths of meaning arranged by the author. <em>Disco Elysium</em> does not escape authorial control, but it places greater weight on the experience of failure. After failure, new dialogue, new shame, and new absurd scenes continue to unfold. The player cannot simply understand failure as a wrong step to be erased before trying again. In this respect, the game approaches an unstable experience of life: things have already happened, and meaning can only continue to form within their consequences.[^17]

This is also what distinguishes it from many traditional narrative games. <em>Disco Elysium</em> does not steadily guide the player toward a predetermined value system. It constantly offers choices, and just as constantly undermines the certainty behind those choices. Different players can read different things from the game, and these readings may contradict one another while still being valid. In other words, the game's meaning is not announced once and for all by the author at the ending. It is generated through the player's repeated failures, misunderstandings, choices, and self-projections. Roland Barthes's idea of "the death of the author" roughly points to the same process: the text no longer obeys only the creator's single intention, and meaning no longer has only one correct answer. It is produced again and again through the reader's, or player's, act of reading and experience.[^18]

The game's anti-traditional narrative is therefore not merely an attempt to be novel. It works because it comes closer to real experience itself. Real life often does not unfold according to dramatic structure: effort does not necessarily bring a clear reward, conflict does not necessarily lead to reconciliation, and truth does not necessarily restore order. Disappointment, accident, misunderstanding, contingency, and anti-drama are part of reality. In this sense, the aim of the game is not to create an alternative reality for the player. It does not allow the player to temporarily escape into a world with more order, justice, and explanatory power. Instead, it lets the player encounter the instability of reality again inside fiction. For precisely this reason, the narrative philosophy of <em>Disco Elysium</em> naturally points toward its concern with the real world: why does the game understand politics, failure, and meaning in this way?

> "Some developers would say, 'There is no politics in our game.' That itself is an extremely radical pro-status-quo political position. For us, failing to depict the class contradiction and ideological conflict of Martinaise would be like failing to depict the air there. You cannot pretend those things do not exist unless you live in a completely vacuum-sealed bubble."
>
> "Some developers would say, 'There is no politics in our game.' That itself is an extremely radical, pro-status quo political stance. For us, failing to depict the class struggle and ideological conflicts of Martinez would be like failing to depict the air there. You cannot pretend those things don't exist unless you live in a complete vacuum."

## IV. The Real World

Although <em>Disco Elysium</em> is boldly fictional, it remains deeply rooted in real experience. Its core creator, Robert Kurvitz, was born in Tallinn, Estonia, in 1984 and grew up during the historical rupture before and after the collapse of the Soviet Union. In his youth he played punk music, read Marxism, and immersed himself in post-Soviet urban experience. ZA/UM, the Estonian studio that developed <em>Disco Elysium</em>, takes its name from the concept of "zaum," the transrational language of the Russian avant-garde.[^19] Early ZA/UM was closer to a literary and artistic collective than to a mature commercial company. Its members tried to find expression through fiction, music, painting, and worldbuilding, but for a long time failed to reach a broad audience. Later, the video game became the medium best able to contain this world. In 2019, <em>Disco Elysium</em> was released and achieved international recognition.

{% include figure.liquid path="/assets/img/user/blog/disco/08.jpg" alt="Linnahall in Tallinn, Estonia, a real post-Soviet ruin and abandoned Moscow Olympics structure" caption="Linnahall in Tallinn, Estonia, a real post-Soviet ruin and abandoned Moscow Olympics structure" loading="lazy" zoomable=true %}

After success, however, ZA/UM quickly fell into controversies over corporatization, control, and the departure of its creators. By around 2022, several core members who had shaped the worldview, textual character, and visual style of <em>Disco Elysium</em>, including Robert Kurvitz, art director Aleksander Rostov, and writer Helen Hindpere, had left ZA/UM.[^20] Kurvitz and Rostov later publicly claimed that after investors and management took control of the company, they were excluded from daily operations, their employment was terminated, and their access to company information was cut off. ZA/UM management denied financial wrongdoing and in turn accused some former members of mismanagement and misconduct.[^21] Whatever one makes of the competing claims, the dispute at least indicates a crucial fact: the original team that created the spiritual core of <em>Disco Elysium</em> no longer controls ZA/UM or the future of its work. ZA/UM, an idealist artistic collective named after "transreason" and once committed to resisting the poverty of reality through art and imagination, was itself ultimately affected by the logic of modern cultural industry, capital control, and struggles over ownership.[^22] The realism of <em>Disco Elysium</em> therefore extends beyond post-Soviet experience. More broadly, it can also be understood as a miniature of contemporary society.

Standing on the eve of the AGI era, ZA/UM's work and its story make us aware that the problem before us is how to deal with the void of freedom. The development of AI has reactivated the imagination of technological progress. It promises efficiency, abundance, and new freedom, while also bringing anxiety about labor, judgment, and creativity.[^23] Since the Renaissance and the Enlightenment, modern thought has increasingly placed reason, dignity, and creativity at the center of humanity's self-understanding. The Industrial Revolution and modern political institutions strengthened the belief that human beings could continue to progress through institutions and technology. But the wars, totalitarianism, colonial violence, Cold War confrontation, and revolutionary failures of the twentieth century repeatedly damaged this humanist confidence. Political ideals that promised emancipation did not eliminate oppression, and market orders that promised freedom and prosperity did not universally deliver progress. The world is now undergoing even more intense geopolitical conflict and social-environmental turbulence, while populism, extremism, and protectionism have become part of the political background of our age.[^24]

This is why technological optimism has gained such wide appeal. Technological optimism treats technological progress as the main solution to social contradiction, crises of meaning, and uncertainty about the future.[^25] When politics, markets, and public deliberation all appear powerless, technology is easily assigned a function beyond that of a mere tool. AI is especially prone to this: it not only improves productive efficiency, but also begins to affect how human beings understand, judge, create, and define their own value.[^23] Borrowing Erich Fromm's distinction in <em>Escape from Freedom</em>, technological optimism is better at providing "freedom from": freedom from physical labor, information limits, knowledge barriers, and obstacles to choice. But it may not provide "freedom to": the ability of human beings to realize themselves through spontaneous love, creation, and action, and to build active connections with others and the world.[^26] Francis Fukuyama's worry about the "last man" can also be extended to the AI age. As technology replaces more and more labor, judgment, and creativity, human beings may no longer need to struggle for grand beliefs and historical goals; they may also lose ambition, the spirit of risk, and the courage to pay a price for ideals.[^27] This is what I mean here by "the void of freedom": freedoms and tools continue to increase, while shared direction and grounds of value become even more unstable.

In this sense, <em>Disco Elysium</em> offers no salvational plan. It does not resurrect revolution, defend capitalism, or restore order through reason. It simply lets Harry keep waking up among the ruins of Revachol, keep solving the case, keep confronting trauma, poverty, failed history, and a self that is difficult to repair. Yet the game does not remain inside nihilism. It still gives the player one plotline with a romantic color. Early in the game, the player encounters a pair of cryptozoologists searching for a mysterious creature. They believe that an officially unconfirmed giant stick insect lives near the Revachol coast. At first this side story seems like a loser's fixation, an absurd rumor, or another comforting fantasy in a broken world. But near the end of the case, Harry reaches the island and, among reeds and fog, truly sees the giant phasmid. It quietly exists there, radiating a mysterious and almost religious sublimity, proving that the world has not been exhausted by politics, capital, trauma, and reason.[^28]

{% include figure.liquid path="/assets/img/user/blog/disco/06.jpg" alt="Harry and the Insulindian Phasmid" caption="Harry and the Insulindian Phasmid" loading="lazy" zoomable=true %}

For this reason, the hope of <em>Disco Elysium</em> is not a triumphant hope, but a more restrained and existential one: the world has no pre-given meaning, and human beings may never fully repair themselves, but as long as they remain willing to act, to take responsibility, and to begin again, meaning may still be created within the void of freedom.
